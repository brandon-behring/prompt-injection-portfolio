## Blog post — LinkedIn (~550 words)

**Reading the OOD wall more carefully: launching prompt-injection-portfolio (v0.1.0)**

Prompt-injection detectors are easy to demo and hard to trust. They post high accuracy on a benchmark,
then fail in production the moment an attacker controls a piece of the model's context. The
research question I care about is *why* — and whether the failure is something a better detector can fix,
or something structural that detection alone cannot.

Today I'm opening **prompt-injection-portfolio** at v0.1.0: a public, pre-alpha research portfolio that
takes the honest findings of a prior case study and rebuilds them, cleaner, as a falsifiable research
program. It ships as three things at once — a textbook, a set of reproducible experiments, and a
build-in-public log — all under one roof.

**What's in the v0.1.0 snapshot.** A 13-chapter textbook skeleton; a six-lane experiment framework where
each lane is a pre-registered hypothesis with a protocol, a results record, and a decisions log; a
210-entry research dossier spanning five topics (97% body-quote-verified); Docker reproduction; an ethics
and dual-use disclosure; and 55 architecture decision records that show the reasoning, not just the
conclusions.

**The first result — and why it's the interesting one.** Before training anything, I pre-registered a
prediction: rank the indirect-injection attack types by how badly a detector would generalize to each
when it's held out of training. Then I ran the experiment across a rung ladder — TF-IDF, a frozen-embedding
probe, and an end-to-end LoRA fine-tune. The prediction *survived* on the cheap rungs and was **falsified
at the LoRA ceiling**: the per-attack-type wall collapses as model capacity rises, and a small end-to-end
fine-tune detects every held-out attack type near-uniformly. The "OOD wall," on this axis, is a property
of the representation — not the task.

That my own prediction was falsified is the point, not an embarrassment. The rule, the tail sets, and the
rung it would be judged on were all fixed before any model trained, and the verdict could only be written
once the full sweep finished. Pre-registration is what makes a negative result trustworthy.

**What's next.** The finding sharpens the question rather than closing it. The attack-type axis is
capacity-dependent; the *carrier* axis — the trust boundary between the model's instructions and the
untrusted content it reads — is the wall the geometry says actually dominates, and it's the next thing I'll
measure. The roadmap (Lanes 2–5: training data, structural defenses, score fusion, activation probes)
re-points around that distinction.

**How to engage.** The book is at [BOOK_URL]; the baselines reproduce from a clean T0; and findings are
open for critique on GitHub Discussions. The repo is pre-alpha and will stay that way until the
experimental program closes — you're watching the work happen, not reading a finished paper. I'd rather be
corrected early than be confidently wrong late.
